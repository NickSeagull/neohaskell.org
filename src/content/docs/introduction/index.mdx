A developer's work begins with understanding what people need to accomplish and which promises a business makes. Knowledge of APIs and syntax helps turn that understanding into software. The harder contribution is often asking the uncomfortable question: what happens when the business cannot keep a promise, who may make an exception, and which part of the process nobody has agreed on yet?

AI can generate an implementation, suggest alternatives, and help explore unfamiliar code. It cannot resolve a disagreement about how the business should work merely by producing a program that builds. The developer still has to map the process, recognize missing decisions, and judge whether the result solves the intended problem.

NeoHaskell is an AI-first language and framework built around that responsibility. Its aim is to make developing software enjoyable: understanding a difficult process, expressing it clearly, and seeing it work as intended. Its conventions and tools shorten the distance between those business decisions and their implementation. The same structure supports reviewing a change, tracing an unexpected result, and deciding what should happen next, whether the code is written with an agent or by hand.

## A shop with three mugs

A shop owner asks for online orders. The shop has three ceramic mugs available. Before choosing a database or generating a checkout screen, several questions affect the design:

- Does adding a mug to a basket make it unavailable to other customers?
- Does the shop promise the mug when it accepts the order or when payment arrives?
- If an order is cancelled, when does its mug become available again?
- What should happen when a customer orders more mugs than remain?

For this first example, accepting an order immediately uses the requested stock. The quantity must be positive, and enough stock must be available. The shop starts with no orders. Accepting an order for one mug leaves two available.

## Why NeoHaskell makes technical choices

NeoHaskell makes several recurring architecture choices in advance. It keeps a history of business changes, separates the decision to accept a change from work with external systems, and provides a common structure for changing and reading application state. The starting point is the shop's behavior; the framework supplies the path for recording its accepted changes and keeping its answers up to date.

A team can build the same approach with another language and selected libraries. It must then choose and maintain the conventions that join those parts. NeoHaskell makes those choices part of the framework, so a developer or agent does not have to invent a new arrangement for each feature.

For example, adding a second interface should reuse the shop's stock policy. The framework already gives that policy a place shared by its interfaces. A developer or agent can concentrate on the condition to check and its expected result. Storage configuration, permissions, and the business policy still need deliberate choices; the conventions reduce the surrounding assembly work.
